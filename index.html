Mechanical Engineering Internship
Automatic Systems Incorporated · Summer 2026

Before this internship, my idea of a conveyor was a moving belt you put your groceries on in the checkout aisle. However, the term encompasses many other types of systems that I am now familiar with. ASI designs and manufactures material-handling systems used in automotive manufacturing plants and airport baggage handling systems. This summer I got the opportunity to work with multiple systems in both categories.

Automotive Manufacturing & Conveyor Systems

The majority of my internship I was working with overhead rail conveyor systems, specifically Power and Free systems. As shown in the name, this system uses a continuously moving power chain to propel trolleys along a separate free, or unpowered, track. The power chain engages the trolleys through attachments called dogs, allowing the trolleys to be propelled, stopped, accumulated and restarted on their own as they move through the system. Miles and miles of these rails can be installed in one automotive manufacturing plant, allowing car parts to be transported between different areas of the facility throughout the process of assembling the car.

General Motors — Arlington Assembly

One of the first jobs I worked on during my internship was for General Motors' Arlington Assembly plant, a 6-million-square-foot facility that produces more than 1,300 vehicles per day. For this project, I modeled and created engineering drawings for various track components, including sections with horizontal turns, vertical curves, and return tracks.

Working on these components gave me a better understanding of how relatively simple-looking sections of track become much more complicated when they have to function as part of a continuous conveyor system. Each section has to interface correctly with the surrounding track while maintaining the geometry necessary for the trolley to travel through the system.

General Motors — Fairfax Assembly

I also spent a large portion of my internship creating track and assembly components for GM's Fairfax Assembly plant in Kansas City, Kansas, which I had the opportunity to visit at the end of my internship.

For this project, I modified standard ASI track components to incorporate additional mechanisms required by the plant's specific conveyor layout. Two components I worked with were trolley stops and anti-backup devices.

The trolley stop allows a trolley to disengage from the moving power chain when an assembly needs to pause at a particular location. The trolley can then remain stationary while the rest of the conveyor system continues operating. The anti-backup mechanism prevents the trolley from drifting backward while it is disengaged from the power chain.

Airport Baggage Handling Systems (ABHS)

Near the end of my summer, I shifted from automotive conveyors to airport baggage handling systems (ABHS). ABHS is a newer area of ASI compared to the automotive conveyor systems that have been a core part of the company for over 50 years. Because the system is still being standardized, there was a lot of work involved in creating standard components, assemblies, drawings, and templates that could be reused across different projects.

During the final weeks of my internship, I focused heavily on creating Inventor drawing templates for baggage handling conveyor queues. I created template drawings for both incline and decline queue configurations covering angles from 18 to -18 degrees, along with drawings for their associated subassemblies.

Once a standard drawing was created, iLogic could then be used to automate the creation of each queue variation and its respective drawing. By entering parameters such as the desired angle and belt length, iLogic could alter our models, assemblies, and drawings to generate the appropriate configuration. This was particularly useful for tedious repetitive tasks. For example, each angle variation frame had a unique sheet metal pattern, so rather than redefining the bends for each piece of metal, the program automatically adjusts each dimension on its own.

Legacy Design & Engineering Systems

The work I did at ASI encompassed a lot of things I had not encountered before. One of my most common tasks was taking older engineering drawings and rebuilding them into Inventor CAD models. Many of these drawings were decades old and originally created in AutoCAD, and they were often difficult to interpret because an entire assembly, including its subassemblies and individual parts, had to fit on a single sheet. I would interpret the drawings, recreate the individual components in Inventor, and rebuild the assembly while updating it to current company standards. This included replacing obsolete components, correcting dimensions and tolerances, and making necessary design changes. One example was a skillet subassembly where several components had been incorrectly drawn or named and right-hand parts had been used where left-hand parts were required. I corrected the assembly, applied the proper constraints, and replaced the encoder with a newer, stronger version.

I also became familiar with Bills of Materials (BOMs), Autodesk Vault, and Fusion Manage PLM. Vault's check-in/check-out system taught me the importance of careful file management, while the PLM process showed me how engineering changes are reviewed and approved by operations, project management, and senior engineers. This work taught me that engineering is about more than creating a correct CAD model; small details in parts, drawings, and documentation can have a much larger impact on the final assembly.

Engineering Graphics TA
ME 212 · Kansas State University · Spring 2026

As a Teaching Associate for K-State's entry-level course in engineering graphics and 3D modeling, I developed strong proficiency in multiple CAD and modeling programs while teaching students the fundamentals of mechanical design. Mechanical Design & Build
Wildcat Combat Robotics · 2026–Present

This is a newer extracurricular for me, but I have already been able to get hands-on experience in mechanical design and fabrication. I work on the design team under my team lead, contributing to the CAD and mechanical development of our competition robot. I am also helping with the electrical side of the robot and have been learning how to solder and work with its electrical components.

The season has just begun, and our initial CAD has been developed in Onshape. Since the robot will go through assembly and testing before competition, we are designing with the expectation that components will need to be modified as we identify problems and improve the design. I am learning how to approach CAD as an iterative process rather than treating the initial model as a finished design.

Our robot is an eggbeater-style combat robot, a common battlebot archetype that uses two vertical spinning weapons mounted within a wide, low-profile chassis. We plan to use a combination of TPU, titanium, aluminum, and other materials, along with commercially available components. As the season progresses, I will be involved in the transition from our initial CAD design to fabrication, assembly, testing, and redesign.
